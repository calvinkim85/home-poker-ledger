How many players

Six, nine, or somewhere between
← Back to the ledger

Guides · Setting up

The honest answer is six to eight. Below that the game thins out, above it people spend the evening watching — and the number changes how the poker plays far more than most hosts expect.

What each table size feels like

  • 3–4 players. Fast, loose, and you are in most hands whether you want to be. Fine for an hour, tiring for four.
  • 5–6 players. Genuinely good poker. Enough hands to stay involved, enough players for position to matter.
  • 7–8 players. The classic home game. More waiting, more conversation, which is usually the point.
  • 9–10 players. A full table. Hands take a while and the blinds come round slowly, so quiet players can go half an hour without a decision.
  • 11 or more. Two tables, or a tournament where people combine as players bust.

The practical ceiling is your table

Nine players need real elbow room, somewhere to put a drink, and enough space that nobody is sharing a corner. A dining table that seats eight for dinner seats about six comfortably for poker, because chips and cards need more room than plates.

Check the physical seats before you check the guest list.

When more people say yes than you planned for

This is a nice problem and it has three reasonable answers.

  • Run a tournament instead. It absorbs a larger field naturally, and as players bust the table shrinks to a good size on its own. More on choosing the format here.
  • Stagger arrivals. In a cash game people can join and leave through the night, so ten invitations rarely means ten simultaneous players.
  • Say no, kindly. A cramped game that runs late is worse for everyone than a good game somebody missed.

Invite more than you need

Expect one or two to drop out. If you want seven at the table, ask nine. Every regular host learns this, usually by ending up four-handed on a Friday.

When the table shrinks mid-night

Home games rarely end with everyone still seated. People leave, and a nine-handed game becomes five-handed by midnight — which is a genuinely different game, usually a livelier one.

Two things are worth agreeing in advance. First, whether there is a point at which the game simply stops: four players who all want to keep going is fine, but three reluctant ones is how a night limps to an end. Second, that anyone leaving early cashes out properly rather than pushing their chips across the table — an untracked stack is the most common reason a count fails to reconcile. Counting a leaver down takes a minute and saves the argument.

How the count affects the money

Two things change with table size, and both are worth knowing before you set the stakes.

More players means bigger pots relative to the blinds, because more people are contributing to each one. A $20 buy-in plays differently nine-handed than it does five-handed.

It also means a longer count at the end. Nine stacks take real time to count down properly, which is an argument for keeping the number of denominations small.
