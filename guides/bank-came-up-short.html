When the bank comes up short

Who eats the difference
← Back to the ledger

Guides · Settling up

You have counted twice and the chips still do not match the money that went in. Before anything else: this is normal, it happens to careful hosts, and the order you do the next three things in matters more than the amount.

Say it out loud, immediately

The instinct is to quietly make it work while everyone puts their shoes on. Resist it. Announce the number before any money moves — “we are eleven dollars short, give me a minute” — because a discrepancy discovered by the host and stated openly is a bookkeeping problem, and the identical discrepancy discovered by a player afterwards is an accusation.

It also stops the settlement proceeding on numbers you already know are wrong, which is how you end up unwinding payments as well as hunting an error.

Look for the cause before deciding who pays

Most shortfalls are one of four things, and they are worth checking in this order, because that is roughly how common they are: a rebuy nobody wrote down, a player who cashed out early and left, a miscount on a single stack, and chips on the floor or in a pocket.

An unrecorded rebuy is by far the most likely, and it has a signature: the gap is usually a round number and usually equals somebody's standard buy-in. If you are short exactly one buy-in, you are almost certainly not short at all — you are missing a line in the ledger. The five ways a settlement goes wrong covers the rest.

Who absorbs it

Once you have genuinely looked and the gap is still there, someone has to take it, and the answer depends on how your game handles money.

If you bank the game — players hand you cash and you hand back chips — the settled convention among hosts is that the host eats it. Not because the host was necessarily at fault, but because the alternative is worse: spreading a small loss across everyone turns your bookkeeping error into a tax on people who came to play cards, and it is the single fastest way to make a good player stop coming. The amount is almost always small. Pay it and move on.

If nobody is banking and players settle with each other, there is no host holding cash to absorb anything, so the gap has to land somewhere explicit. The defensible answer is the players who finished ahead: they are the ones with winnings to give up, and a dent in a good night costs less than deepening someone's loss. The calculator will do this for you — Cover the gap from the top — starting with the biggest winner and chopping between the leaders when they finished close enough that one paying alone would change the order.

What not to do

Do not let one player volunteer to absorb it to end the evening. It feels generous and it is genuinely corrosive: the error is still there, nobody decided anything, and that player now has a quiet grievance. Do not split it equally either — that charges the people who already lost money for a mistake in the accounts.

And do not adjust a cash-out until the numbers fit. That is the one move that makes the shortfall permanently unfindable.

Then make it not happen again

One banker, not two. Every exchange done on the table where people can see it, not in a doorway. Chips handed over only after the money is actually in hand. And each buy-in written down as it happens rather than reconstructed at the end — the pause costs fifteen seconds and removes the most common cause entirely.

Keep the settlement afterwards too. A dated record of a night that came up short is worth more than one that balanced, because it is the night somebody will remember differently in a month. Being the banker covers the rest of the job.
