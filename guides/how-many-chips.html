How many chips you actually need

Buying a set
← Back to the ledger

Guides · Setting up

A chip set is bought once and lived with for years, so it is worth thirty seconds of arithmetic before ordering. The good news is the answer is boring: most home games need a 500-chip set and nothing more.

The rule of thumb

Roughly 75 to 100 chips per seated player. That sounds generous until you remember that rebuys come out of the same box and that a player with forty chips in front of them can actually make a bet without counting.

  • Up to 5 players: a 300-chip set is genuinely enough.
  • 6 to 8 players: 500 chips. This is the size most home games should buy, and the size most sets are sold in.
  • 9 or more, or tournaments with rebuys: 1,000 chips, mostly so you are not colouring up constantly.

The denomination that runs out

Every game discovers this the same way: you have plenty of chips and none of the one you need. It is always the workhorse — the denomination that pays the blinds and makes the small bets.

At a $20 buy-in with 10c/20c blinds, that is your 25c chip, and you will want something like 200 of them across the table while the $5 and $25 chips sit in the rack barely touched. When you buy, weight the order heavily toward the bottom two denominations. A set split evenly across five colours looks tidy in the case and plays badly.

There is more on picking the values themselves here — the counts above only make sense once the denominations fit your buy-in.

Leave a reserve in the rack

Do not distribute the whole set. Starting stacks should come out of maybe two-thirds of what you own, with the rest staying in the box for rebuys and colour-ups.

A game that has physically run out of chips has to stop and redistribute, usually mid-hand, usually while someone is all in. That is a worse problem than owning slightly more plastic than you strictly need.

Three colours beat five

The instinct is to use every colour in the case. Resist it. Three denominations in play covers almost any cash game, reads instantly across the table, and makes the count at the end of the night dramatically faster.

Every extra colour is another thing to identify at cash-out, when everyone is tired and wants to go home.

What about weight and material?

It changes how the game feels and nothing about how it plays. Heavier clay composite chips stack and shuffle better than light plastic ones, which matters more than people expect over a four-hour session — but it has no bearing on how many you need.

Buy the count first, then decide what you want them made of.

A quick sanity check

Before the first game, deal out every starting stack onto the table at once. If the box is empty, you bought too few. If every player can comfortably make a three-chip bet without breaking a larger denomination, you bought correctly.
