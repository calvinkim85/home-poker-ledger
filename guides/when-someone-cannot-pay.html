When someone can't pay

Handling a shortfall
← Back to the ledger

Guides · Settling up

Someone at the table cannot cover what they owe. It is the most uncomfortable moment a home game produces, and it is almost entirely preventable by decisions made hours earlier.

Prevent it: collect at the start

The single measure that removes this problem is taking the initial buy-in before the first hand. Someone who cannot cover the buy-in finds out while everyone is still standing around, which is awkward for a moment. The alternative is finding out at 2am when they are down three buy-ins, which is awkward for a friendship.

If your group settles by transfer rather than cash, collect the record up front even when the money moves later: names entered, buy-ins logged, before a card is dealt.

Cap rebuys for the same reason

Unlimited rebuys are how a $20 night becomes a $200 one without anyone deciding it should. A limit — two rebuys, or a midnight cutoff — is not about distrust. It is a guardrail that protects the person most likely to need it and least likely to ask.

When it happens anyway

Deal with it as a group, at the table, before anyone leaves. The worst version is the host quietly covering it and nobody else knowing, because the debt still exists but now it is invisible and attached to a friendship.

Write it down as a debt, not as a shortfall

The person still owes what they owe. Record it explicitly — who owes whom, how much, agreed when — and settle the rest of the table normally. This is by far the cleanest option, because it keeps the arithmetic honest. The books balance; one payment is simply outstanding.

What not to do: make the numbers fit

The tempting move is for someone to say “call mine $30 less” so the totals reconcile and everyone can go home. It feels generous and it is a mistake. One player has silently absorbed the whole thing without the table deciding that was fair, and next week nobody remembers it happened.

If somebody genuinely wants to forgive part of a debt, that is a decision worth making out loud, not disguised as a chip count.

If they are short at cash-out rather than short on the buy-in

This is a different problem and often not a payment problem at all. A player whose chips do not match what they should have usually means a miscount somewhere at the table, not dishonesty. Recount before you conclude anything.

The structural fix, if it recurs

Once is an accident. Twice with the same person is information.

  • Lower the stakes. If someone cannot comfortably cover a night, the game is priced above its table.
  • Pay in before playing, no exceptions. Applied to everyone, it singles nobody out.
  • Cap the rebuys.

None of that requires a conversation about anyone's finances, which is the point. A rule that applies to the whole table lets someone step back from a stake without having to explain why.

Keep the record either way

Whatever the table decides, write down what was agreed — the amount, who it is owed to, and when it is expected. Not because anyone is untrustworthy, but because memory is, and an unwritten debt between friends turns into a disagreement about what was said.

The calculator will tell you plainly when the money on the table does not match the money that went in, and by how much. That number is where this conversation should start.
