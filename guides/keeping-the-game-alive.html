Why home games die

And how to keep yours going
← Back to the ledger

Guides · Running the game

Home games rarely end in an argument. They fade: a cancellation, a month off, a date nobody set, and then it has been since spring. The causes are predictable, which means most of them are avoidable.

1. The date stops being fixed

The most common killer, and the most fixable. A game on a rule — first Friday, every other Wednesday — survives. A game that needs a date agreed each time eventually fails to find one.

Post the next date at the end of the current night, while everyone is still in the room and in a good mood.

2. The stakes drift up

It happens gradually and with good intentions: the game feels flat, someone suggests raising the buy-in, nobody objects out loud. A month later one or two people are quietly not coming.

Almost nobody says “this is too expensive for me.” They say they are busy. If attendance drops after a stakes change, that is your answer — and lowering them again is a table decision that singles nobody out.

3. It runs too late

A game with no finish time is a game people with jobs stop attending. Agree a last hand — “last hand at midnight” — and actually honour it.

Ending while everyone still wants another hour is the version people come back to.

4. The settle-up is a chore

This one is underrated. If the night reliably ends with twenty minutes of arithmetic, a disagreement about a rebuy, and somebody owed money until next week, the whole evening acquires a bad ending — and people remember endings.

Collect buy-ins up front, record every rebuy as it happens, count down properly, and settle before anyone leaves. A five-minute finish is worth more to attendance than anything else you can change. There is a guide on doing the count cleanly.

What keeps one going

  • One person who owns it. Games with a host survive; games that belong to everyone belong to nobody.
  • A group chat with the date in it.
  • A slightly bigger list than the table. Ten names for eight seats absorbs cancellations.
  • Stakes at the low end of comfortable. You want people playing because it is fun, not because they can afford it.
  • Beginners made welcome. Every game needs new people eventually.

If it has already stopped

Pick a date, send one message, do not apologise for the gap. Most dormant games restart on a single invitation, because everyone assumed somebody else had lost interest.
